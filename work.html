CONTEXTAmbia is Elige's mobile calling and eSIM app. AmbiaPay is its wallet for sending money across borders and paying M-PESA bills.
PROBLEMKenyans abroad send money home all the time, and when a payment fails it costs them money and trust at once.
IDENTIFYI defined who we were building for and benchmarked competitors so leadership had facts for the commercial talks.
TRANSLATEI turned requirements into user stories and acceptance criteria and sketched the screens in Figma.
WORK ON ITI tested the integrations with our payment partners, ran the beta myself, and negotiated with vendors, including the move from OTP to PIN.
A DECISION THE DATA MADECards from outside Africa kept being declined. That evidence is why we changed card provider.
SHIPI made the go or no-go call on releases.
OUTCOME1,500+ users in under three months.
WHAT I TOOK FROM ITThe biggest lesson I took from the AmbiaPay launch is that a product can be technically ready but still fall short if its payment experience doesn't meet customers' needs. Identifying card-payment limitations affecting users outside Africa taught me to look beyond individual transaction failures, investigate the underlying product gaps and work with partners to find solutions that support a better customer experience.

HOW WE WORKEDI wrote the brief, set the art direction and approved every page. Claude was my design and build partner: it drafted the screens and code, and I chose and refined the direction. Where a page had no V2 yet, it produced three options and I picked one.
DECISIONS I STAND BEHIND
  • I put all three products on Services and let the third card peek, so people know the page continues.
  • I removed the "For you" section because it repeated the home page, and kept the airtime balance on Calls, where it belongs.
  • I moved the design from cold blue to a warm cream, with one colour per product.
  • I kept every call to action. I would not trade accessibility for a cleaner look.
  • I put support in the header and at the bottom of the page, because a floating button would have covered the cards.
  • I rebuilt the eSIM package screen to hold up to 12 packages in one section, because the first design couldn't.
IDENTIFYI mapped user journeys in Mixpanel by the buttons people tapped, and looked for where they dropped off. People couldn't see the feature.
WORK ON ITI led the fix on three fronts: a redesign, a better place for it in the app, and targeted marketing.
OUTCOMEClick-throughs to the eSIM page rose by about 45%, measured by comparing two Mixpanel reports before and after. It was not an A/B test, and it counts clicks, not purchases.
WHAT I TOOK FROM ITThis experience reinforced that product value depends not only on what a feature does, but also on how easily users can discover and access it. It taught me to look beyond functionality, use behavioural data to challenge assumptions about the user experience, and recognise discoverability as an important part of product adoption.
CONTEXTThe renal department at Kenyatta National Hospital, Kenya's national referral hospital, moved onto Helium's electronic document system.
PROBLEMThe Renal Department lacked a centralised digital records system, limiting efficient patient information management, retrieval and integration with existing hospital systems.
WORK ON ITMigrated 670+ patient records with quality checks on everything scanned and moved, and built the system to work with the hospital's existing records system.
SHIPTrained 20+ clinical and technical staff, then handed over with full documentation and six months of operations support.
OUTCOMEDelivered on schedule, with training, handover and a support plan in place.
WHAT I TOOK FROM ITThe biggest lesson I took from this project is that successful technical delivery goes beyond building software; it's about making it work within the organisation's operational environment. At KNH, that meant accounting for existing HMIS workflows, coordinating integration requirements, migrating 670+ patient records and preparing users for adoption. It reinforced that my responsibility as a project lead doesn't end when the technology is ready, but when the organisation is ready to use and sustain it.
IDENTIFYRan discovery calls with facilities in Kenya, Uganda and Rwanda, and sat in Ministry of Health workshops on Kenya's digital health plans.
TRANSLATETurned those conversations into requirements and acceptance criteria, and sorted the backlog into epics, features and user stories every sprint.
SHIPClosed out each sprint as the final regression check.
OUTCOMEMy input helped prioritise the insurance billing integration and the national health data work. I shaped what engineering saw, but I didn't own the company roadmap.
WHAT I TOOK FROM ITThe biggest lesson I took from contributing to the product roadmap at Helium Health was the importance of bridging the gap between regional customer needs and engineering priorities. As the East African affiliate representative, I gathered feedback through quarterly check-ins, stakeholder discussions and operational support, translated recurring challenges into product requirements, and worked with engineering to prioritise and clarify what needed to be addressed. This taught me how to turn customer feedback into actionable product input while balancing local needs with the wider product direction.
